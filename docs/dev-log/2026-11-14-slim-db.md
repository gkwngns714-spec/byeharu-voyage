### The lap close, and the lap table

`cmd.standing_route_close_lap` reads its own lap, that lap's orders and the one lap before it: it does **not** read other laps. What it
writes is the cost. Its UPDATE sets `closed_at`, an indexed column, so the new row version takes a new entry in every one of the table's seven indexes; it
deletes the lap past the keep; it empties `trades` on the lap seven back (another row, another round of index entries). About twenty
random index pages a close, on a table that is not in memory: the 8,233 ms of three closes in the sampled beat.

The table is 372 MB for **about 87,000 rows** (886 routes at the keep of 100; the planner's 188,000 to 250,000 was stale). The heap is
226 MB, the toast 29 MB, the seven indexes 99 MB. A row is not 1.5 KB wide: its fixed columns are about 170 bytes, `skipped` (each
refused line with its sentence) averages 256, `trades` 170 on the 7 % of laps that carry it. About 40 MB of rows lie in 255 MB of heap
and toast: every lap row is updated five to ten times while it is open (stops done, skipped lines) on pages that are full, so each
version goes elsewhere. The space is the bloat, and the fix for it is a rewrite (RUNBOOK), not retention.

A merchant closes about 68 laps a day (87,000 rows span about 35 hours), so "100 laps" is a day and a half. What the game reads of a
merchant's laps: the last 24 hours and the lap before them (`route_earnings`, `world.fleet_trades`, `stats_merchants_window`); the
newest 20 inside three days (`npc_share_budget`); the newest 8 (the fleet card), 2 (`npc_leg_lines`), 1 (the close, the rest timer).
The losing-laps rule is the players' alone. The trades payload **is** read for merchants (the fleet card shows the newest seven laps'
trades), so it stays; it is 7 % of the rows.

### #1 The drift: one harbour in ten a run

`drift_spread_runs` 10 (1 = as before). The job still runs every minute; `public.drift_ports_due` gives it the harbours whose turn the
minute is, by their place in the port order: 26 or 27 of 263 a run, every harbour once in ten runs. A harbour stepped after k missed
slots takes **one compounded step**:

    drift <- theta^k x drift + sigma x sqrt((1 - theta^(2k)) / (1 - theta^2)) x N(0,1)

That is the law of k one-slot steps of the walk as it stands (proof 108: 1,887 rows fifty slots behind come out with a spread of 0.0467
against the law's 0.0459; one slot behind 0.0200 against 0.0200). So a price moves as far in a game day as it does today.
`drift_sigma` and `drift_theta` are not touched: the walk is **no stronger and no gentler**. The other builder's finding (sigma 0.03 a
60-second slot moves a price a mean 7 % in ten minutes against a 5 % margin) is neither helped nor hurt: over ten minutes a harbour
now moves once, by the same distance. If the owner wants the walk gentler, the knob is still `drift_sigma`.

Stated per game day, as the day is about to become an hour: a harbour nobody looks at is repriced
`game_day_seconds / (60 x drift_spread_runs)` times a game day. Six times at a one-hour day; once in ten game-days at the 60-second
day prod serves now. The stock's regeneration and the glut's decay were already closed-form over the days and hours missed, so they
are right at any day length; nothing in the hunk assumes one.

What a player sees: nothing new. A quay somebody opens is stepped on that read (`world.market` calls the step for its harbour), every
minute as now. What a merchant sees: the price she plans on is the price she trades on; it is up to ten minutes old at a harbour
nobody has opened. This **alters live play** in that one respect and wants its 30-minute after-check.
