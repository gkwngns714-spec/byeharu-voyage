# 2026-11-14: a game day lasts one hour (0412)

The owner, 2026-11-14: **"change the game day - 1 hour."** He had been told a game day is 12 real minutes, and that a longer day means fewer events per real minute on a database that is too small.

Decided by default (the coordinator, the same day): the day he sees goes from 12 minutes to 60, a factor of 5, and sailing keeps its proportion. Every decision below is marked **(default)** and can be reversed.

## What it does to play, first

A voyage is the same number of days and costs the same. It takes five times as long on the wall clock.

| First voyages from Lisbon (a new company's own ship, 16.2 knots) | miles | voyage-days | before | after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Setubal | 38 | 0.10 | 9 s | 45 s |
| Porto | 158 | 0.41 | 36 s | 3.0 min |
| Seville | 284 | 0.73 | 66 s | 5.5 min |

No first voyage is longer than a sitting. A sea delay is longer too: one calm costs 1 to 4 voyage-days, which was 1.5 to 6 minutes and is 7.5 to 30 minutes.

## The first finding: there was no one day

"A game day is 12 minutes" was true of one thing only: the clock in the top bar and the Sun on the map. Reading the code found four different days, none derived from another on the server.

| The "day" | Length before | Where it lived | What runs on it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The clock's day (top bar hour, the Sun) | 720 s | a **client constant only** (`GAME_CLOCK_RATE = TIME_COMPRESSION / 8` in `src/lib/format/time.ts`); the server had no such number | the hour shown, day and night on the map |
| The date's day (top bar date) | 2,880 s | a client constant (a month is one real day) | the date shown |
| A voyage-day | 90 s | `world_config.time_compression` 960 (86,400 / 960) | sailing, supplies, wages, weather and raid rolls, every "N days" on a screen |
| The market's day | 60 s on prod (2,880 in the chain; the owner set 60 by hand on 2026-10-30) | `world_config.game_day_seconds` | stock regeneration, daily trade caps, requests' deadlines, a route's laps per day |

So the 12-minute day was eight voyage-days, and the client also carried its own copy of the 960. The coordinator's note said the sailing day is `game_day_seconds` (60 s): it is not. `game_day_seconds` is the market's day. A fleet settles one voyage-day every 90 seconds.

## The one authority now

- `world_config.time_compression` stays the one rate. Every sailing function already reads it, and `world.snapshot()` already serves it. **960 -> 192.**
- `world_config.clock_voyage_days` (new, 8) states the ratio the client only assumed. The day's length is **derived, never typed**: 86,400 x 8 / 192 = **3,600 real seconds** (it was 720). A voyage-day is 450 seconds (it was 90).
- `world_config.clock_anchor` (new) holds where the clock stood at the switch, so the date and hour do not jump.
- `public.clock_set(compression, at)` (new, owner only) is the one door that moves the rate. It moves the knob, the ships at sea and the anchor together.
- The client reads all three from the snapshot (`setServedClock` in `src/lib/format/time.ts`). Its own 960 and its own 8 are now only what is drawn before the first snapshot arrives; `tests/rpc.surface.spec.ts` holds them equal to the served values.

"Game time" rows keep their game values and take five times as long on the wall clock. "Real time" rows are unchanged.

## The traps, and what was done

**(a) Fleets already at sea.** A voyage is a pure function of its departure, its arrival and the rate, so just changing the rate would throw every ship back to a fifth of her progress. `public.clock_set` re-times each voyage under way about the switch instant: `t' = at + (t - at) x 5` on both `departed_at` and `eta`. Her miles, her settled days and her next day boundary are identical at the instant; what is left of the passage takes five times as long. No ship moves on the chart, none arrives early, none is stranded. Proven on probe voyages in the migration and in proof 110 (a plain voyage, a five-day voyage switched in her third day, a voyage held by a battle).
- Cost of that choice: `departed_at` of a voyage under way at the switch is no longer her true departure (it is earlier). "At sea since" reads long for those voyages until they land, and a skill trained in the hours before she really sailed may not count for the rest of that one passage.
- A battle's hold running across the switch keeps its end; that battle runs its full length (an early end no longer finds the hold by its start). Ten minutes at most, once.
- A rest or repair already running keeps its stored end.

**(b) The calendar must not jump.** The anchor stores the clock's reading at the switch: whole days are the date as it was printed, the fraction is the hour as it stood. `tests/format.spec.ts` holds that the date, the hour and the Sun are the same at the instant before and after, and that the date then turns exactly when the hour passes midnight.

**(c) Nothing pays five days at once or skips a day.** Voyage days: proof 110 `DH_DAYS` (2 days complete before and after; settling at the switch settles nothing; exactly one day settles at the new boundary). Market days, real days: not moved, so nothing to skip.

**(d) Rates per real time.** The Sun follows the served clock. **Price drift, production and population were NOT slowed.** `game_day_seconds` and `drift_slot_seconds` are counters of the form `floor(epoch / knob)` stored in a dozen columns (`port_goods.last_regen_day`, `drift_slot`, `trade_contracts.expires_day`, `trade_daily.game_day`, ...). Multiplying the knob by five divides the counter by five: regeneration and drift would stop until the count caught up (years), and open requests would never expire. The by-hand move of 2026-10-30 broke dividends that way (0312). It needs its own migration with an offset, and its own measured window. Also, 0409 (database slimming) re-cuts the drift bodies and derives its step from those knobs, so they were left alone here.
- What that means until it is done: per voyage-day the market now regenerates five times as much stock and drifts for five times as many slots as before. A price that moved 7 % in ten real minutes moved that much over 6.7 voyage-days; it now moves that much over 1.3 voyage-days.

**(e) The economy's balance.** Confirmed unchanged in game terms: wages, supplies, wear and raid rolls are per voyage-day; dock fees are per call; a passage is the same number of days; so cost and profit per voyage are what they were. **What does not keep its ratio** (all because real-time things stayed real): income per real hour falls to about a fifth for everyone, while navy upkeep, dividends (9 % a real day), the loot cap, daily caps and skill training stay per real time. So upkeep weighs five times as much against trade income, a daily cap is five times as loose per voyage, and skills train five times as fast per voyage. The market side is in (d). If the owner wants those to follow the clock too, each is one knob.

**(f) A new player's first hour.** See the table at the top: 45 seconds to 5.5 minutes for a first voyage.

## Known risks

- **The browser specs that sail a real voyage take five times as long.** `tests/arrivalWait.fixture.ts` now reads the rate from the authority (its bound is still ten voyage-days: 75 minutes, paid only by a failing run). Specs with their own fixed waits may go red on main's Acceptance run; a single calm on a near trip is now 7.5 to 30 minutes.
- Between the migration's deploy and the client's publish (minutes), an open browser still assumes 960: the real time beside "N days" reads a fifth of the truth, and the hour hand runs fast, until it reloads.
- The market's clock is not slowed (above). That is the next slice if the owner wants the whole game five times slower, and likely the larger saving.

## To reverse

`select public.clock_set(960);` (owner only) puts sailing and the clock back, continuously. The date and hour stay one clock.
